# Measure the work. See the change.

A good training record answers two different questions. **What did I do?** Your sets, repetitions, loads and sessions answer that. **What seems to be changing?** A series of consistently taken photos can add a visual perspective. PGYM puts both next to the proof of each workout, so progress is something you can revisit instead of trying to remember.

The AI analysis and coaching feature is optional and part of PGYM Pro. Everyone can see the invitation on Today. If you are not subscribed, tapping it shows the Pro plans. Photo analysis is available only to eligible adults who turn it on. Your workout proof and ordinary share card do not depend on it.

## Take a photo you can compare later

Finish your workout in PGYM, then tap **Finish and take proof**. Take the photo in the app camera, not from your photo library. Fit your whole body in the frame, including your head and feet. Stand naturally, face the camera, keep your arms slightly away from your torso, and use even light and a plain background when you can. Avoid a mirror angle that cuts off your legs or changes your proportions.

Wear clothing you are comfortable being photographed in. Shorts can help show the legs; if comfortable, a sports bra and shorts or shorts without a shirt can make more contours visible. You do not need to undress to earn a proof. If clothing obscures a dimension, the model may be unable to score the image reliably.

For comparison photos, try to repeat the same stance, camera height, distance, light and clothing. Morning photos under similar conditions may be easier to compare, but **the workout proof is taken when you finish training**. A post-workout pump, hydration and lighting can move the appearance of a score. Do not retake a proof to chase a higher number.

Choose a card filter and size, then tap **Stamp it**. Once the server stamps the workout, open its card detail page and tap **Get AI analysis and coaching**. If you have Pro but have not enabled analysis, PGYM asks for consent first. It sends a reduced copy of that workout's original photo for one analysis request; the server does not store the image. If the original photo has been removed from your phone, that card cannot be analyzed again.

## Read the seven scores

The result is six visual dimensions and a separate **overall fitness and muscle score**, each from 0 to 100. They are AI estimates for this photo, not measured anatomical ratios or a health grade. Open **What do these scores mean?** on the card, or **What do the six scores mean?** in score history, for the in-app explanations.

| Dimension | What the score looks at | What it cannot tell you |
|---|---|---|
| Shoulder-to-Waist Ratio (V-Taper Index) | Visible shoulder width relative to waist width. | A camera angle or pose can change the outline. |
| Waist-to-Height Ratio (Metabolic & Fat-Loss Velocity) | Visible waist width relative to full-body height. | It does not measure visceral fat, metabolism or body composition, despite the label. |
| Torso Taper Angle & Lateral Delt Cap Curvature | Torso taper and the shoulder contour. | A single view cannot isolate muscle growth from pose, clothing or light. |
| Bilateral Symmetry & Postural Alignment Score | Left and right outlines and visible landmark alignment. | It cannot diagnose posture, joint health or injury risk. |
| Upper-Torso & Core Definition Score (Conditioning) | Visible upper-torso and core separation. | Shadows, a pump and temporary changes can alter the result. |
| Quad-to-Hip & Knee Proportionality (Lower-Body Sweep) | Thigh contour relative to hips and knees. | Shorts, stance and lens perspective affect it. |

The overall score is a seventh estimate, not the arithmetic average of the six. A score of 70 does not mean 70% healthy or 70% fit. Its most useful comparison is with **your own** earlier photos taken in similar conditions. The card detail also shows three short coaching sentences and one encouraging sentence. Treat the coaching as a prompt for your next session or a reminder to keep photos consistent, not as a diagnosis or a personalized medical prescription.

## Use the chart without chasing noise

Open **Progress**, then **Score history**. Select **Overall fitness and muscle** or any of the six dimensions in the Metric menu. Each point is one scored workout photo. The line is the average of scored photos in the preceding seven days. PGYM also keeps your highest recorded value for each dimension and your best overall score.

Look for a pattern across several weeks, not a one-point jump. If the line changes but the photos were taken in different light or clothes, consider the capture conditions before changing your training. A personal record is the highest AI estimate you have recorded, not a validated physical measurement. Compare it with your workout log: Are you completing the planned sessions? Are your working sets, repetitions or loads changing? If a lift stalls, that is a reason to review the plan and recovery, not proof that a photo score is wrong.

This is why the two records belong together. The workout log tracks your training input and performance. The photo series offers a noisy view of visible output. Neither alone establishes why your body changed, and PGYM has not clinically validated its seven scores against body composition or muscle measurements.

## Choose what goes on the card

From a stamped workout's detail page, tap **Share again** and choose **Workout stats**, **Stats + scores**, or **Scores only**. The first is the ordinary proof card and contains no AI score. The other two render the seven scores over the original local photo. The coaching text stays on your private card detail page. PGYM opens the iOS share sheet only after you choose a layout; you decide whether to post, send privately or save the image. If the original photo is gone from your phone, score layouts cannot be rebuilt, but the ordinary workout card remains available.

Sharing can make a workout feel more tangible and invite encouragement from people you trust. It is optional. You do not need a public post or a public score to get the value of a consistent record. Choose the audience and layout that make you want to keep training, and remember that a social post cannot validate the AI estimate.

## What the evidence supports

Resistance training is the intervention that can improve strength and muscle size; a photo or score does not cause those adaptations. A [systematic review and network meta-analysis of resistance-training trials](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37414459/) found that the studied training prescriptions improved strength and hypertrophy relative to no exercise. In a [10-week randomized study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38286426/), progressing either load or repetitions increased leg-extension strength and muscle cross-sectional area in previously untrained adults. Logging the actual sets gives you a way to see whether the training stimulus is progressing.

Feedback and self-monitoring can help people stay active, but the evidence does not establish that PGYM's specific photo scores improve training outcomes. A [systematic review of physical-activity monitors](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8791066/) found increases in activity, with cautions about study quality and publication bias. That evidence concerns activity monitors, not AI physique scoring. A [study of a 2D image system](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38282462/) found that camera and background changes could shift body-composition estimates. PGYM's scores are a different, unvalidated measure, so standardizing your photo is a practical way to reduce noise, not a guarantee of accuracy.

Public sharing may provide support, but it is not a proven shortcut to muscle growth. A [randomized trial of social-network features in a walking program](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5127765/) found similar activity effects across its intervention groups. We offer the share card because some people value documenting their work and inviting support. Keep the private record if that serves you better.

**Measure the work. Watch the longer trend. Share on your own terms.** Start with a workout, one proof photo and the next session you can repeat.
